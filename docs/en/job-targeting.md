# Turn One Job Description into an Evidence-Based Interview Campaign

> **Best for:** readers with a target role who are still preparing from a generic question bank<br>
> **First pass:** 15 minutes<br>
> **Output:** a one-page role brief, an evidence matrix, and ten scored practice sessions

## A job description is evidence, not an exam syllabus

A job description mixes role scope, recruiting language, required constraints, preferences, and an idealized candidate. Copying every noun into a study list creates broad familiarity but weak interview evidence.

Use the posting to form bounded hypotheses:

1. What customer workflow must change?
2. What is the primary engineering spine: AI systems, data infrastructure, regulated deployment, migration, or another domain?
3. Which production responsibilities are explicit?
4. What evidence would make each capability observable?
5. Which interview formats are plausible, and which details still require recruiter confirmation?

Never turn a public responsibility into a claim about a company's private interview loop.

## Keep three columns separate

| Type | Example | Safe conclusion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Public fact | The posting owns discovery, build, rollout, and adoption | The public scope is end to end |
| Practice hypothesis | End-to-end ownership may be tested in project depth or system design | Prepare both and confirm the current loop |
| Personal evidence | You owned synchronization, rollout, and recovery | Show the decision, artifact, result, and boundary |

This separation prevents two common credibility failures: presenting community reports as company facts, and presenting study exercises as production experience.

## Extract six signals at most

Record the URL and checked date, then extract only:

- outcome verbs such as discover, build, deploy, adopt, measure, or enable;
- the primary engineering systems;
- production responsibility such as evaluation, security, rollback, or handoff;
- customer constraints such as industry, language, travel, or deployment boundary;
- leverage signals such as reusable components or product feedback;
- hard constraints and preferences.

If the role brief cannot fit on one page, it is not yet a brief.

## Translate requirements into observable evidence

Use this chain for every important signal:

```text
public job signal
-> responsibility or risk in the real work
-> behavior an interviewer could observe
-> personal evidence you can defend
-> boundary that appears under follow-up
```

A strong evidence item has six layers:

| Layer | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Context | Who was making what decision in which workflow? |
| Baseline | What time, quality, risk, or manual cost existed before? |
| Decision | What trade-off did you personally own? |
| Artifact | What code, test, diagram, evaluation, trace, or record exists? |
| Result | What changed in system or user behavior? |
| Boundary | What was not yours, what remains unsolved, and what would you change? |

Without a decision, the story is a project summary. Without an artifact, it is hard to verify. Without a boundary, it often sounds inflated.

## Label evidence honestly

- **D — Direct:** you owned this responsibility in real work or a credible maintained project.
- **A — Adjacent:** the context differed, but the underlying responsibility transfers.
- **E — Exercise:** a synthetic or public design exercise; useful reasoning, not production experience.
- **G — Gap:** no defensible evidence yet.

Adjacent evidence can be strong. A production workflow with identity, idempotency, approval, and compensation may transfer to agent tooling. State both what transfers and what remains new. Do not repaint A or E as D.

## Build hypotheses about interview surfaces

| Public signal | Plausible practice surface | Artifact | Confirm with recruiting |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| End-to-end delivery | Project depth or system design | User-to-rollout evidence flow | Does design include AI or data? |
| Hands-on coding | Algorithms, applied build, or debugging | Timed implementation and tests | Which format and language? |
| Embedded customer work | Discovery or role play | Refusal, alternative, next-step recording | How much solution detail is expected? |
| Evaluation, security, audit | Reliability or security review | Slices, threat model, release gates | Is there a dedicated review? |
| Reusable field learning | Manager or behavioral depth | Field-to-product decision memo | Is cross-customer leverage assessed? |

Update the plan when recruiting provides new information. Do not defend an outdated preparation guide against first-party facts.

## Use ten output-producing sessions

1. Record a 90-second role positioning statement.
2. Run a ten-minute depth interview on the strongest project.
3. Complete one timed coding or debugging task in the target spine.
4. Design one workflow-first system.
5. Run discovery without proposing a solution too early.
6. Handle one high-pressure customer request.
7. Diagnose one production incident with versioned evidence.
8. Complete one full mock loop.
9. Rehearse only the two highest-risk behaviors.
10. Run a second blind score and decide whether to apply, build evidence, or retarget.

Reading without an output, score, and second attempt does not count as a session.

## Apply or close the gap?

Do not use a mechanical match percentage. A missing preferred tool may be minor when the underlying mechanisms are direct evidence. A hard location, work-authorization, language, customer-travel, or hands-on coding constraint may be decisive.

You can often apply while learning when:

- the missing item is a preference or tool and the mechanism is already proven;
- your domain differs but you can show how you learn workflows and responsibility boundaries;
- the interview format is unfamiliar but the underlying capability is strong.

Pause or retarget when:

- the role requires production coding and you can only present architecture;
- the operating model conflicts with real travel, language, or authorization constraints;
- every relevant story is confidential and cannot survive safe follow-up;
- model demos are your only evidence for a role that owns data, permissions, rollout, and incidents.
